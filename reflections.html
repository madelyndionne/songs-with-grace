Devotional

Reflections

A mirror has a perfectly reasonable job. We stand before it because we want to see what other people will see. Is my hair in place? Does this shirt look right? Is there something on my face? A mirror allows us to step outside ourselves for a moment and see our appearance from another perspective. But somewhere along the way, we can begin asking the mirror questions it was never designed to answer. Am I beautiful? Am I acceptable? Am I enough? Am I useful? Who am I? And the mirror cannot possibly know.

What the Mirror Cannot See

The song, Reflections, begins with a woman doing something most of us have done thousands of times: “Looking in a polished mirror / Wondering who I've become / Seeing all the little changes / Since the day my life began.” Age has changed the face looking back at her. Life has changed far more. And almost immediately she recognizes something important: “But the face before me isn't / All there ever was to see...”

Of course it isn't. The mirror can show the lines on her face, but it cannot show what put them there. It can show the shape of her mouth, but it cannot show the words she chose not to say. It can show the eyes, but it cannot show what those eyes have witnessed, whom they have wept for, what they have forgiven, or how many times they have looked toward God for help. The mirror can reflect a body. It cannot reflect a life.

Yet there is another kind of mirror we carry with us. It is built gradually—a comment someone made when we were young, a rejection we never quite understood, a failure, a comparison, a criticism that hurt enough to remain, a compliment we could never quite believe, our own fears, our own regrets, our own expectations. Piece by piece, those experiences can construct an image within us.

The song describes it this way: “I have gathered many pictures / Of words said throughout the years. / Some were painted using kindness, / Others borrowed from my fears.” And then comes the question: “Yet I wonder if that portrait / Comes close to the one He knows.” That may be one of the most important questions we can ask about ourselves.

When Reflection Becomes Identity

A reflection does not have to be completely false to become distorted. The woman on the cover really does have the small imperfections of an ordinary human body. But the mirror exaggerates them. A slight roll becomes larger. A jowl becomes heavier. Hair becomes less attractive. Color becomes duller. The reflection has taken something real and made it disproportionately important. Our internal mirrors can do exactly the same thing.

Perhaps we failed at something, and the mirror says, You are a failure. Perhaps someone rejected us, and the mirror says, You are unwanted. Perhaps age has changed what we can do, and the mirror says, You are no longer useful. Perhaps fear stopped us once, and the mirror says, You are a coward. Perhaps someone criticized something we offered sincerely, and the mirror says, Never offer it again. There may be a fragment of experience behind each conclusion, but the conclusion can become a grotesque enlargement of the fragment.

And if we stare at that reflection long enough, we may begin living according to it. That is where distorted self-perception becomes spiritually consequential. A false reflection can make us timid. It can keep us wounded. It can cause us to hide gifts God intended us to use. It can make us refuse opportunities because we have already decided we are inadequate. It can make us so preoccupied with how we appear to others that we become unavailable to serve them. And it can cause us to spend years protecting an identity that God never gave us.

What God Sees

Scripture provides a profoundly different perspective. When Samuel looked at Jesse's sons, he naturally noticed outward appearance. God corrected his perspective: human beings look upon the outward appearance, but the LORD looks upon the heart. (1 Samuel 16:7) That does not mean God overlooks what is wrong within us. Quite the opposite.

God sees more accurately than we do. Psalm 139 describes a God who searches and knows us—our thoughts, our ways, our words, our movements. David does not respond by hiding from that knowledge. He ultimately invites God to search him, reveal what is wrong, and lead him in the everlasting way. (Psalm 139:1–6, 23–24)

God's knowledge is not flattering, but neither is it distorted. He sees accurately. He sees the weakness accurately. He sees the strength accurately. He sees the sin accurately. He sees the wound accurately. He sees the gift accurately. He sees the fear accurately. He sees the possibility accurately. He sees what needs repentance and what needs healing. He knows what should be surrendered and what should finally be allowed to grow.

That is why the answer to a distorted self-image is not simply better self-esteem. It is truth. We do not need to replace “I am terrible” with “I am wonderful.” We need to replace distortion with what God says is true.

Paul warned against thinking of ourselves more highly than we ought, but he did not tell believers to think of themselves as worthless. Instead, he called for sober judgment—and then immediately spoke about the different gifts God gives His people and their responsibility to use them. (Romans 12:3–8) That balance matters. Humility is not thinking as little of ourselves as possible. Humility is being willing to accept the truth about ourselves before God—and then allowing Him to use what He has given us.

Scripture says that believers are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works. (Ephesians 2:10) That means distorted self-perception can become more than a private struggle. It can interfere with service.

If God has equipped us to encourage, but our reflection tells us nobody wants to hear from us, someone may go unencouraged. If God has given us something to create, but our reflection tells us it could never matter, something may remain uncreated. If God has given us wisdom earned through years of walking with Him, but our reflection says age has made us irrelevant, something valuable may remain unspoken. If God opens a door and our reflection says, “Someone like you doesn't belong there,” we may walk away from an opportunity God intended us to enter.

Fear does not get to determine our usefulness. God has not given His people a spirit of fear, but of power, love, and a sound mind. (2 Timothy 1:7)

Stepping Away From the Mirror

There is nothing wrong with looking into one. The danger begins when we give the reflection authority it does not possess. A mirror can tell us something about how we look. It cannot tell us who we are. It certainly cannot tell us what God can do with a life surrendered to Him.

The song, Reflections, says: “There's a place no mirror reaches, / Hidden far from human eyes. / Yet the One who knows me deepest / Never needed my disguise.” There is enormous freedom in that.

God already knows. There is no carefully arranged version of ourselves that fools Him. There is no flaw the mirror can discover that He somehow missed. There is no hidden wound He failed to notice. There is no gift buried so deeply that He cannot see it. And there is no need to exaggerate either our greatness or our inadequacy before Him. We can simply come truthfully.

Paul wrote that now we see through a glass darkly, but one day we will know far more fully. (1 Corinthians 13:12) For now, every human reflection is incomplete—including our own. Perhaps that is why we must be careful about allowing reflections to become verdicts.

The mirror tells a story. Other people tell stories about us. Our memories tell stories. Our fears tell stories. Even our own minds tell stories. Some contain truth. Some contain distortion. None tells it all.

So when the reflection says, You are too old. Too damaged. Too ordinary. Too frightened. Too late. Too insignificant, perhaps we should remember who is speaking. It is only the mirror. And the mirror does not know enough to render the verdict.

The One who formed us does. The One who searches the heart does. The One who knows what needs changing does. The One who knows what He has placed within us does. And the One who knows us most completely is also the One who can make our lives fruitful in His service.

The goal is not to learn to admire our reflection. It is to stop giving the reflection authority that belongs to God. Then perhaps we can finally step away from the mirror—not proud of ourselves, not ashamed of ourselves, but available. Available to be corrected. Available to be healed. Available to grow. Available to serve. Available to become useful in the hands of the One who sees beyond every reflection—to the soul behind the wall.

Prayer

Father, You know me more completely than I know myself. You see what is good and what is broken, what needs repentance and what needs healing, the gifts I recognize and those I have been afraid to use. Show me where the reflection I carry has become distorted by fear, criticism, comparison, failure, or my own judgment. Keep me from thinking either too highly or too little of myself. Teach me instead to believe what You say is true.

When an old reflection tells me I am unworthy, incapable, or no longer useful, keep me from giving it authority that belongs to You. Search me, correct me, heal me, and make me willing to use whatever You have entrusted to me for Your purposes. Let me step away from the mirror and become increasingly available to You. In Jesus' name, Amen.
