Devotional

Willow Water

Some plants seem remarkably willing to begin again.

A willow branch can be cut from the tree that sustained it, separated from the roots that once supplied it, and placed somewhere entirely new. To the eye, it may look like little more than a severed branch.

But the story is not necessarily over.

Something is already present within it that can contribute to the formation of new roots.

That ordinary observation gives the song, Willow Water, its central image:

*“What is living deep within
The willow helps new roots begin.”*

There is something profoundly hopeful about those words.

Because there are seasons in human life when we can feel much like the cutting.

Something familiar has been severed. A relationship ends. Someone we love dies. Health changes. A place that once felt like home no longer exists. A disappointment alters the future we expected. Sometimes there is no dramatic event at all—we simply realize that the ground beneath us has changed.

And we wonder: *How do I grow from here?*

For the Christian, the answer begins with something far greater than our own ability to recover.

Jesus promised His followers that the Holy Spirit would dwell with them and be *in them*. (John 14:16–17) Scripture describes the same Spirit who raised Christ from the dead as dwelling in believers. (Romans 8:11)

That means when circumstances leave us feeling uprooted, we have not been emptied of the presence of God.

*Someone is living within.*

The Spirit does not merely visit us when life is flourishing and withdraw when we are broken. He remains present in the confusing places, the wounded places, and the seasons when new growth has not yet appeared.

Roots usually begin where nobody can see them.

That may be one of the most important lessons in the song.

We often look for evidence of God's work above the surface. We want the new leaves. We want visible growth. We want circumstances repaired and prayers answered in ways we can recognize.

But God may first be working underneath.

Strengthening. Deepening. Teaching. Correcting. Comforting. Establishing roots where there were none before.

Paul prayed that believers would be strengthened by God's Spirit in the inner person and become *rooted and grounded in love*. (Ephesians 3:16–19) Colossians similarly describes the Christian life as being rooted and built up in Christ. (Colossians 2:6–7)

Roots are not glamorous. But they are what allow growth to remain when the wind comes.

And then the willow gives us another part of the picture. What it carries can be shared.

That is where the metaphor becomes especially beautiful.

The song, Willow Water, moves from the willow forming its own roots to something from the willow being used in the care of another cutting:

*“As what the willow freely shares
With other cuttings needing care,
The Spirit works through us for others—
Extending healing through our care.”*

Scripture gives us a remarkably similar pattern. God comforts us in our trouble, and that comfort equips us to comfort others in theirs. (2 Corinthians 1:3–4)

We receive—and then we give. We are strengthened—and then we strengthen. Someone helps carry us—and eventually we find ourselves strong enough to help carry someone else. (Galatians 6:2)

The pain itself is not what qualifies us. *God's work within the pain does.*

A person who has grieved may recognize grief in another person's eyes before anyone else notices. Someone who has known loneliness may understand the importance of simply staying. Someone who has struggled with doubt may know how to sit beside a questioning person without becoming frightened by the questions. Someone who has received mercy may become unusually generous in giving it.

And someone who once wondered whether they would ever have roots again may someday become part of the way God helps another person find solid ground.

We should be careful not to carry the willow metaphor farther than Scripture allows. The Holy Spirit is not an impersonal substance or spiritual growth compound. He is God, dwelling within His people. Nor does Scripture promise that every wound will disappear in this life.

The willow is only an illustration. *Scripture is the authority.*

But the illustration points toward something Scripture makes wonderfully clear: God places His Spirit within His people. He strengthens us inwardly. He roots us more deeply in Christ. And He can work through what He has done in us to minister to someone else.

That changes how we might look at our broken places.

Perhaps the question is not only: *“Will I ever grow again?”*

Perhaps, in time, another question emerges: *“How might God use what He has grown in me to help someone else?”*

The final chorus of the song changes the language:

*“Living deep within—
The Spirit helps new roots begin.
Healing what’s been broken,
Helping all of us root again.”*

There is the deeper truth.

We do not manufacture the life. We do not create the Spirit's power. We do not become someone else's Savior. We simply remain available to the God who lives within us.

And sometimes the person who once needed help putting down new roots becomes the very person God uses to help another begin.

Prayer

Father, thank You for giving Your Spirit to dwell within Your people. When I feel uprooted, broken, or uncertain of where I belong, remind me that Your presence has not left me. Strengthen me in the places others cannot see and root me more deeply in Christ and in Your love. Bring healing according to Your wisdom, and use even the difficult places in my life for Your purposes. As You comfort, strengthen, and restore me, make me willing to share that comfort with others. Help me carry burdens gently, love faithfully, and never forget that the healing power belongs to You. May what You grow within me become a blessing to someone else who is trying to find roots again. In Jesus’ name, Amen.

Digging Deeper — Botanical Note

Willow is well known for its ability to root readily. Willow tissue contains biologically active compounds, including indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), a naturally occurring auxin involved in plant growth and root initiation, and salicylic acid-related compounds. Homemade willow extracts are used by gardeners as a rooting aid, although their effectiveness varies with plant species, concentration, preparation, and season.
