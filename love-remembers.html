Devotional

Love Remembers

Memory is one of the ways we hold our lives together. We remember names, faces, places, stories, promises, laughter, grief. Memory helps us know where we have been and who has walked beside us. And then, sometimes, memory begins to loosen its grip.

The song, Love Remembers, enters that painful place gently: “You ask me if we've met before...” Few questions can carry so much ache. A person who once knew us instantly may look at us with uncertainty. Shared history still exists, but the path back to it has become difficult to find. Yet almost immediately, Scripture gives us something firmer than human memory on which to stand.

The God Who Does Not Forget

God's knowledge of us does not depend upon our ability to remember Him perfectly. Psalm 139 describes a God who already knows us completely—our thoughts, our movements, our words, our ways. (Psalm 139:1–6) Isaiah gives one of Scripture's most tender pictures of divine remembrance. Even if a mother could somehow forget the child she bore, God declares that He will not forget His people. (Isaiah 49:15–16)

Paul writes that even when we are faithless, He remains faithful because He cannot deny Himself. (2 Timothy 2:13) And Romans 8 declares that nothing in all creation can separate those who are in Christ from the love of God. (Romans 8:38–39) Memory loss does not fall outside that promise.

Our ability to remember God may become fragile. God's remembrance of us does not.

That truth changes how we look at the person whose memory is failing. Loss of memory is not loss of worth. She is still herself—still deserving of patience, dignity, and tenderness; still known by God; still loved.

Love Carries What Memory Cannot

The chorus says: “When you forget, that’s my clue: / Love remembers.” When one person can no longer carry all the memories, others begin carrying them too. They remember the stories, the habits, what made her laugh, what frightened her, what she loved, how she served, and who she was before memory began becoming unreliable. And sometimes they remember aloud—not to test her or force recognition, but to hold continuity around her. That is a profound act of love.

The song, Love Remembers, includes a wonderfully ordinary memory: “You once walked barefoot in the rain, / Laughed so hard you peed in shame...” Love does not remember only the grand occasions. It remembers the ridiculous ones, the embarrassing ones, the tiny moments that would never appear in a biography but somehow belong unmistakably to that person. Love remembers the whole person. And when memory fades, love keeps saying: You were here. You mattered. You are still known.

Staying Becomes Love

The song says: “Still I stay—'cause deep inside, / I know you’re there...” Sometimes love cannot repair what has been lost. It can only remain present. There may be no restored memory, no breakthrough, no moment when everything suddenly becomes as it once was. There may simply be another visit, another hand held, another familiar song played, another answer repeated, another meal shared, another quiet hour together.

Scripture's description of love in 1 Corinthians 13 emphasizes endurance. Love bears, believes, hopes, and endures. Love does not simply disappear when circumstances become difficult. (1 Corinthians 13:7–8, 13) That does not mean caregivers never become exhausted. They do. It does not mean grief disappears because love is present. It does not. Love can be faithful and still tired, faithful and still grieving, faithful and still needing help. But love keeps looking for the person behind the loss.

The family depicted on the cover does not appear to be demanding recognition from the elderly woman. No one is holding up photographs and asking, Do you remember? They are simply near. A hand holds hers. Another rests gently on her shoulder. Their presence says what memory may no longer be able to say: We know who you are. We are still here.

Known Beyond What We Can Recall

The bridge says: “There’ll come a day I’ll fade from you, / When memory fails us both in view. / But if your heart still feels me near— / Know I was always… always here.” There is something deeply human in those words. We want to be remembered. We want the people we love to know who we are. We want shared history to remain shared. But eventually every human memory has limits. Even without dementia, names disappear, dates become uncertain, stories blur, and details slip away.

Christian hope does not finally rest upon human remembrance. It rests upon being known by God. The One who created us does not lose track of us when we become confused. He does not love only the competent version of us. He does not value us only while we can recognize everyone in the room. He knows the person beneath every loss.

And perhaps when we sit beside someone whose memories are fading, we are allowed to participate in that faithfulness in a small human way. We remember. We carry the stories. We protect dignity. We remain. We love the person who is still there even when she cannot always find the path back to us. Because relationship is more than recognition, and love is more than memory.

Human love remembers for one another. God's love never forgets.

Prayer

Father, thank You that Your knowledge of us does not weaken and Your love does not depend upon the strength of our minds. Be near to those whose memories are fading, and surround them with patience, dignity, tenderness, and faithful care. Strengthen the families and caregivers who carry stories, repeat answers, endure grief, and continue showing up in love. When someone I love cannot remember me, help me remember who they are. Keep me from measuring their worth by what they can recall, say, or do.

And when I am afraid of my own frailty, remind me that I am fully known by You. My memory may fail, my strength may diminish, and much of this life may eventually slip beyond my reach—but nothing can remove me from Your faithful love. Thank You that when I cannot hold everything, You still hold me. In Jesus' name, Amen.
