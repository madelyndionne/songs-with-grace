Devotional

Grace That Is Not in Vain

Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 15:10 sit within his testimony about the risen Christ. He remembers his unworthiness, acknowledges the work he has done, and attributes both his standing and his strength to God’s grace. He does not pretend his past was good. He does not claim his labor earned God’s favor. Grace accounts for what he has become, and grace has borne fruit in what he does.

That is the distinction at the heart of this devotional. Grace is not God overlooking sin, nor is it a reward for overcoming sin on our own. It is His undeserved favor, which saves and then works in the person who receives it. The song, Grace, illustrates that movement, but Scripture tells us what grace is and what it does.

A Gift That Cannot Become Wages

Ephesians 2:1–10 begins with our need for life, not with our ability to improve ourselves. God acts in mercy. Salvation is His gift, received through faith; good works follow as the life for which He has made us. Romans 11:6 guards the same boundary: if grace is earned, it ceases to be grace.

This reaches deeper than a warning against boasting. It speaks to the private effort to become enough before coming to God. We may know that salvation is a gift and still live as though each failure puts us back at the beginning, required to purchase our way into His care. But a gift cannot become a debt God owes us, however diligently we work.

The first verse of the song gives that struggle a voice. Scripture supplies the answer. We come to God empty-handed because His mercy, not our adequacy, is our hope.

Mercy Meets Us Truthfully

Grace does not require us to minimize what is wrong. Titus 3:3–7 describes the condition from which God saves and places the cause of salvation in His mercy. Hebrews 4:14–16 directs us toward Him when we need mercy and help. We need neither a polished account of ourselves nor a hopeless one. We need an honest one.

This is why grace can be both humbling and comforting. It removes our claim to have earned God’s favor, but it also removes the burden of pretending we could. We can name a failure without making it the final word about what God is able to do.

The Grace That Saves Also Teaches

Ephesians 2:10 follows Ephesians 2:8–9. The order matters. We are not saved by good works, yet God’s saving work prepares us for them. Titus 2:11–14 describes grace teaching us to turn from ungodliness and live differently. Romans 6:1–14 confronts the thought that grace gives sin a safe place to remain.

Scripture therefore gives us no reason to call a weakness good simply because God is gracious to us. Nor does it tell us to manufacture holiness so that He might finally be gracious. His grace begins the work and teaches us to walk in it.

The song, Grace, illustrates this in its second verse: grace did not leave the singer where she stood. Change may involve repentance, patience, and repeated dependence on God. Its pace does not make sin harmless; its difficulty does not make grace powerless. The question is whether we are yielding to what grace teaches.

Grace at Work Is Still Grace

In 1 Corinthians 15:10, Paul can speak of real labor without taking ultimate credit for it. God’s grace was not in vain in him. Grace did not make his actions meaningless; it made faithful action possible.

This protects us from two opposite errors. We need not treat our work as payment to God. We also need not dismiss obedience as though effort and grace cannot belong together. We can act, serve, repent, and persevere while acknowledging that God is at work in us (Philippians 2:12–13).

The song’s bridge holds those truths together: grace asks no payment, yet changes the heart in which it lives. Its words are an illustration. Paul’s testimony and the wider teaching of Scripture are the ground beneath them.

Mercy Changes Our View of Others

Jesus’ parable in Matthew 18:21–35 asks us to reckon with the mercy we have received when another person needs mercy from us. The passage does not require us to deny wrongdoing. It exposes the hardness of accepting mercy for ourselves while refusing to consider it for someone else.

That work may begin before we say or do anything. It may begin in the way we see. Do we reduce someone to a failure? Do we grant ourselves the benefit of context while denying it to them? Do we ask God for patience with our slow growth while demanding immediate perfection from another?

The third verse of the song, Grace, turns toward that inward place. Its prayer that kindness grow in how I see asks for more than courteous behavior. It asks God to shape the judgment from which our behavior grows.

A Blessing We Cannot Bestow Ourselves

Numbers 6:24–26 records the blessing God gave for His people. Its source and fulfillment belong to Him. We can pray for His gracious care over ourselves and others, but we do not create that grace by speaking of it. The shift in the song’s final chorus—from asking for oneself to wishing grace for another—illustrates a fitting response to mercy received.

We end, then, where we began: dependent on God. His grace is not earned at the start, and we do not outgrow our need for it later. We receive mercy, submit to its teaching, labor with the strength He gives, and ask Him to make us merciful toward others. If grace bears fruit in us, it has not been in vain—and the praise remains His.

For Reflection

Where am I still trying to turn God’s gift into something I can earn? What is His grace teaching me to leave behind or practice faithfully? Is there someone whose need for mercy I see less clearly than my own?

Prayer

Father, I have nothing with which to purchase Your grace. Thank You for meeting my need with mercy through Jesus Christ. Give me the honesty to confess what is wrong without hiding it or calling it good. Teach me to walk in the new life You have given, and keep me dependent on You as I do the work before me.

Where I have mistaken my effort for the source of my worth, humble me. Where I have used grace as an excuse to remain unchanged, correct me. Let Your mercy shape how I see others, especially when kindness is difficult. May the good You bring forth in my life point back to You. Amen.
