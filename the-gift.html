Devotional

The Gift

Scripture describes salvation in unmistakable terms: “For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God”, and then immediately adds, “Not of works, lest any man should boast” (Ephesians 2:8–9). Before we consider anything we might do for God, Scripture establishes what He has done for us. Salvation is His gift.

The song, The Gift, begins with something very ordinary: an old lady spending part of each year preparing things for others. Long before the packages are opened, she has been thinking about the people who will receive them—planning, making, organizing, spending, wrapping, and finally sending. She expects nothing in payment. The gifts were never transactions. But that does not mean she does not care whether they are received.

When no message comes, her “little old lady heart” squeezes a bit. Not because anyone owes her something, but because what she prepared in love has gone out of her hands, and she does not know whether it has truly been received. There is a profound difference between a gift being given and a gift being received.

A Gift We Could Never Earn

The song uses that simple human experience to point toward something infinitely greater—not because our gifts can compare with God's, but because an ordinary thing can sometimes help us understand a spiritual truth. Scripture says, “all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God” and then speaks of being “justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus” (Romans 3:23–24). Romans makes the contrast even sharper: “the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord” (Romans 6:23).

Salvation is not wages for good behavior. It is not payment for service. It cannot be purchased with generosity, church attendance, religious knowledge, or a sufficiently respectable life. We do not accumulate enough merit to make God owe us salvation. We come as people who need grace.

Free Does Not Mean Without Cost

And this Gift was not inexpensive. Scripture says, “God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us” (Romans 5:8). Peter says Christ “his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree” (1 Peter 2:24). The gospel Paul received and preached included the central truth “that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures” and “that he rose again the third day” (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).

That changes the meaning of free. Free does not mean without value. Free does not mean without cost. It means the cost is not being charged to the recipient. Christ bore the cost we could not pay. That is grace.

Given—and Received

Yet a gift may be freely offered and still remain unopened. John says of Christ, “as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name” (John 1:12). Jesus says, “whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life” (John 3:16).

We can know about the Gift. We can admire the wrapping. We can hear other people describe what they have received. We can even understand the theology of salvation remarkably well and still leave the question of Christ at a distance.

Perhaps that is the quiet question beneath the song, The Gift: What are we doing with what God has already offered? For the person who has never received Christ, the invitation remains. Salvation is not something to earn first and receive later. We come with empty hands, trusting Christ.

The Life That Follows the Gift

For those who have already received Him, there is another danger: beginning to live as though we must somehow repay God—working harder, doing more, trying to make ourselves worthy of something that was given precisely because we could never make ourselves worthy of it.

Grace does not leave us unchanged. Titus says, “the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men”, “Teaching us” how to live godly lives (Titus 2:11–12). And immediately after saying salvation is not of works, Ephesians says, “we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works” (Ephesians 2:10).

That order matters. Good works are not the price of the Gift. They are part of the life that follows receiving it. Gratitude, obedience, love, and service do not purchase grace; they grow from grace.

The little package in the song waits for someone to open it. The greater Gift has already been prepared. The price has already been paid. The gospel has already been proclaimed. Nothing owed. Nothing left to earn.

The question is not whether salvation can be earned. Scripture has already answered that. It is the gift of God. The question is whether we will receive Christ by faith—and, having received Him, live gratefully from the grace we were never able to purchase.

Prayer

Father, thank You for the Gift of salvation that I could never earn and could never repay. Thank You for loving me enough to send Your Son and for the price Christ willingly paid for me on the Cross. Help me to receive Your grace with humble faith and to live in gratitude for what You have freely given.

May my obedience, love, and service never become an attempt to earn Your favor, but always be my response to the grace I have received. Keep the gospel clear in my heart: salvation is Your gift, Christ has paid the cost, and my hope rests in Him. In Jesus' name, Amen.
