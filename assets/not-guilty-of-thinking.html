Devotional

Not Guilty Of Thinking

A thought can arrive without permission.

Hebrews 4:15 tells us that Jesus Himself was tempted, yet without sin. That distinction matters. If the presence of temptation were itself sin, those two truths could not stand together. Scripture therefore gives us an important place to begin: being confronted by a thought is not the same as consenting to it.

The song, Not Guilty Of Thinking, compares unwanted thoughts to messages appearing on a computer screen. You did not write the message. You did not request it. Its appearance in your notifications says nothing about what you believe, desire, or intend to do. You may recognize immediately that opening it would be unwise. You can delete it without ever discovering what was inside.

Our minds can work much the same way. A disturbing thought may suddenly appear. Temptation may present an image, possibility, resentment, fear, memory, or desire. Sometimes the very presence of such a thought troubles us: Why did I think that? What does that say about me?

Its arrival alone may say very little.

James 1:14–15 describes a progression. Temptation presents something to desire; desire is then allowed to conceive, and eventually sin is brought forth. There is movement between temptation and sin. We should take temptation seriously, but we need not confuse being tempted with having already surrendered to it.

That is where discernment enters.

We do not control everything that knocks at the door of the mind, but Scripture does teach us to govern what we willingly entertain. Second Corinthians 10:5 speaks of bringing thoughts into captivity to the obedience of Christ. Philippians 4:8 directs our attention toward what is true, honest, just, pure, lovely, and worthy of praise.

That does not require examining every wrong thought until we understand why it appeared. Sometimes examining temptation merely gives it another opportunity to speak.

You don't have to open the message.

There is also a difference between an unwanted thought and one we deliberately invite back. We can rehearse resentment. We can cultivate fantasy. We can feed fear. We can repeatedly return to something we know is drawing us away from God. At that point, we are no longer merely noticing what appeared at the door; we are deciding whether to give it room.

But even when we recognize that we have made the wrong choice, the answer is not hopeless condemnation. Romans 8:1 directs the believer back to Christ. We confess what is truly sin, receive the mercy God has provided, and learn again to choose what belongs in a mind being shaped by Him.

So when an unwanted thought appears, do not automatically pronounce yourself guilty because it arrived.

Recognize it.

Measure it by Scripture.

And when it has no rightful place there—

Delete it and let it go.
