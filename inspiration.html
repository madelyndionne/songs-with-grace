Devotional

Inspiration

Sometimes we enter silence because we want something—an answer, a line of music, a direction, a solution, a word that will finally make sense of what has been circling in the mind. The song, Inspiration, begins there, with a crafter trying to make room for thought: “Quiet now... let the silence speak / There are treasures silence keeps / If I loosen my own control / Something deeper fills my soul.” At first, the desired result still matters. The writer wants words. The mind is searching for meaning. But the song gradually discovers that the product may not be the most important part of the experience. The deeper movement is the process of becoming receptive.

We remove what is extraneous. We quiet competing voices. We stop insisting that the moment must produce a particular result. We examine what rises in the mind and discard what does not belong. We become attentive. That can be one entrance into Christian meditation.

Not an Empty Mind, but an Attentive One

Biblical meditation is not simply the attempt to make the mind blank. Scripture repeatedly directs meditation toward God, His Word, His works, and His ways. The blessed person delights “in the law of the LORD; and in his law doth he meditate day and night” (Psalm 1:2). The psalmist says, “I will meditate also of all thy work, and talk of thy doings” (Psalm 77:12), and again, “I will meditate in thy precepts” (Psalm 119:15). The goal is not mental emptiness for its own sake. It is attention directed toward God and His truth.

There are many ways a believer may arrive at that attentive place. Scripture, prayer, worship, music, solitude, walking, grief, or ordinary work may each become occasions in which the noise recedes and our attention turns more deliberately toward Christ. The song illustrates one such path. It does not prescribe a formula. The common movement may be simpler: less distraction, less insistence on controlling the experience, more deliberate attention to God, and greater willingness to listen.

Releasing the Product

One of the most revealing lines in the song, Inspiration, is: “The crafter seems to have less say.” The crafter has not disappeared. There is still thought, judgment, skill, choice, and discernment. But the need to dominate the process begins to loosen.

That resembles an important movement in prayer and meditation. We may begin by asking God for an answer, an outcome, or a particular experience. Yet relationship becomes more important than the thing we hoped to obtain. Jesus says, “Abide in me, and I in you” and “without me ye can do nothing” (John 15:4–5). The branch does not manufacture fruit by straining harder. It remains in the vine, and fruit grows from that living relationship. That does not mean effort is unnecessary. It means relationship comes before production. The question changes from What will I get from this time? to Whom am I attending to?

Listening and Discerning

The song also includes an essential safeguard: “Examining and discarding / Waiting for something to grow / Then listening and disarming.” Quietness does not make every thought trustworthy. An inward impression is not automatically the voice of God simply because it arose during prayer or meditation.

Scripture says, “Prove all things; hold fast that which is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21), and “believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God” (1 John 4:1). God's written Word remains the governing authority. Jesus calls the Holy Ghost “the Spirit of truth” (John 14:17), and says that He will “bring all things to your remembrance, whatsoever I have said unto you” (John 14:26). The Spirit of truth does not contradict the truth God has already given. Christian listening therefore includes discernment. We listen humbly, compare what we perceive with Scripture, and remain willing to release what does not withstand that test.

From Something to Some One

Then the final chorus changes only a few words, but those words reveal where the entire process has been leading: “Quiet now... let the Spirit speak / There are treasures His Spirit keeps / If I loosen my own control / Some One deeper fills my soul.” At first, the singer was waiting for something deeper. By the end, the discovery is Some One.

That is the difference between using silence merely as a technique and entering quietness as a posture of relationship. The goal is not to create a mystical experience. We cannot manufacture intimacy with Christ by arranging the perfect atmosphere. Nor do we summon the Holy Spirit by becoming sufficiently quiet. Jesus promised of the Spirit, “he dwelleth with you, and shall be in you” (John 14:17), and Paul writes, “the Spirit of God dwell in you” (Romans 8:9).

But we can become more attentive to the presence and truth we so easily overlook when the mind is crowded with urgency, noise, and our own determination to control what happens next. Perhaps this is why stillness can feel like an arrival even though we have gone nowhere. We have not brought Christ closer. We have become quieter before Him.

And sometimes, after loosening our grip on the product we thought we needed, we discover that the deeper gift was not the answer, the insight, or even the song. It was intimacy with the One we were learning to hear.

Prayer

Father, quiet in me what keeps me from attending to You. Help me release the need to control every moment or demand a particular result from my time with You. Teach me to meditate on Your Word, to remember Your works, and to listen with humility. Give me discernment to test every thought and impression by the truth You have already given in Scripture. Keep me from seeking an experience more than I seek Christ. As the unnecessary noise falls away, deepen my awareness of Your presence and draw me into a closer, steadier relationship with You. In Jesus' name, Amen.
