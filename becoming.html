In so many ways, I’ve broken—

Sometimes loudly, sometimes low.

Bent beneath the weight of grieving,

Moved through fires I did not show.


But mercy found the hollow spots

Where memory had bled,

And placed within those emptied rooms

A seed of life instead.

Being broken is becoming—

I’m still becoming Me.

Bruised beneath a smiling face.

Hidden soft in sacred space.


But even when I came undone,

The Maker knew my name—

And gathered every scattered piece

To start my life again.

It wasn’t strength that made me stand,

Or will that kept me true—

The touch of something vast

That held when I withdrew.


The love that stitched my story back

With thread I couldn’t see,

And whispered through my silence,

“You are still becoming Me.”

Devotional

Becoming

There are times when becoming seems to begin with breaking.

Something happens that changes us. Grief bends us. Disappointment cracks something we thought was secure. Pain settles into places no one else can see. We continue functioning, perhaps even smiling, while carrying wounds quietly beneath the surface.

And naturally, when healing begins, we may think the goal is to become ourselves again.

The song, Becoming, begins there.

“Not yet the one I’m meant to be,
I’m still becoming Me.”

There is hope in those words. Brokenness is not the end of the story. God does not abandon His children among the scattered pieces. Scripture describes Him as the Potter and us as the clay, still subject to His hands and His purposes. (Jeremiah 18:1–6; Isaiah 64:8)

But as the song continues, something changes.

The person who thought she was becoming herself hears another voice:

“You are still becoming Me.”

That changes the destination.

Perhaps we thought God’s work was primarily about making us happier, stronger, more confident, less wounded, or more like the person we believe we were supposed to become.

God certainly heals. He restores. He strengthens. He comforts.

But Scripture reveals something greater at the center of Christian transformation.

God is conforming us to the image of His Son. (Romans 8:29)

We are being changed into His image through the work of the Spirit. (2 Corinthians 3:18) The old ways are being put off while the new person is being renewed according to the One who created us. (Ephesians 4:22–24; Colossians 3:9–10)

That means becoming is not merely improvement.

It is transformation.

And transformation can reach places that simple repair never could.

The song speaks of hollow places, scars, silence, ashes, and roots beneath a tree. Those images matter because much of God’s work occurs where other people cannot see it.

A tree does not begin with the branches everyone admires. Beneath the ground is an unseen system of roots—growing, reaching, strengthening, drawing what is needed for the life that eventually appears above.

Our spiritual lives can be much the same.

God may be doing some of His deepest work in seasons when we appear to be accomplishing very little. He can use suffering to produce endurance, deepen dependence upon Him, expose what is false, and turn our attention toward what is eternal. (Romans 5:3–5; James 1:2–4; 2 Corinthians 4:16–18)

That does not mean every wound was sent by God or that suffering itself is inherently good.

It means suffering does not place us beyond His redemptive reach.

Even there, the Potter still has the clay.

And gradually another truth becomes apparent: some things we once thought were part of us were never meant to remain.

Pride can fall.

Self-reliance can fall.

Bitterness can fall.

The identities we constructed around our wounds can fall.

The need to control everything can fall.

The false things can fall.

And this brings us to the final movement of the song:

“And when all that’s false has fallen,
What remains of me is... He.”

Scripture does not teach that becoming like Christ means that our individual personhood disappears. God does not erase the person He created.

He transforms us.

Paul could say, “Christ liveth in me,” while still speaking of the life he now lived by faith. (Galatians 2:20) The remarkable mystery of Christian transformation is not that the person vanishes, but that Christ becomes increasingly evident through that person’s life.

His love begins replacing our selfishness.

His mercy begins replacing our hardness.

His patience begins challenging our impatience.

His humility confronts our pride.

His faithfulness reshapes our choices.

His character becomes increasingly visible in ours.

And suddenly the three statements within the song reveal an entire spiritual journey.

I’m still becoming Me.

That is where we may think the journey is headed.

You are still becoming Me.

That is where Christ reveals the deeper purpose.

What remains of me is... He.

That is where we finally understand.

The Christian life was never merely a project to construct a better version of ourselves.

It is the lifelong work of God, faithfully continued in us, forming us increasingly into the likeness of Christ. (Philippians 1:6; Romans 8:29)

We are not finished.

We are not beyond repair.

And our broken places do not have the authority to determine what we will become.

We are clay in holy hands.

We are still being formed.

We are still being changed.

We are still becoming.

Prayer

Father, I place every part of myself into Your hands—the parts I understand and the parts that still feel broken. Thank You that You have not abandoned the work You began in me. Show me what needs to be healed, what needs to be surrendered, and what needs to fall away. Keep me from seeking merely to become a better version of myself when Your purpose is so much greater. By Your Spirit, conform me increasingly to the image of Your Son. Let His love, mercy, humility, faithfulness, and grace become more visible in me. And through every season of my life, continue Your work until what others increasingly see in me is Christ. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
