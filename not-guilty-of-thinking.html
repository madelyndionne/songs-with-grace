Devotional

Not Guilty Of Thinking

A thought can arrive without permission. Scripture tells us that Jesus “was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin” (Hebrews 4:15). That distinction matters. If the presence of temptation were itself sin, those two truths could not stand together. Scripture therefore gives us an important place to begin: being confronted by temptation is not the same as consenting to it.

The song, Not Guilty Of Thinking, compares unwanted thoughts to messages appearing on a computer screen. You did not write the message. You did not request it. Its appearance in your notifications does not by itself establish what you believe, desire, or intend to do. You may recognize immediately that opening it would be unwise. You can delete it without ever discovering what was inside.

When Something Appears at the Door

Our minds can work much the same way. A disturbing thought may suddenly appear. Temptation may present an image, possibility, resentment, fear, memory, or desire. Sometimes the very presence of such a thought troubles us: Why did I think that? What does that say about me? Its arrival alone may say very little.

James describes a progression: a person is “drawn away of his own lust, and enticed”; then, “when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin” (James 1:14–15). Scripture does not tell us to treat temptation casually, but neither does it collapse temptation and sin into the same thing. There is a movement from what entices us toward what we receive, cultivate, and act upon.

Not Every Thought Deserves a Place

That is where discernment enters. We do not control everything that knocks at the door of the mind, but Scripture does teach us to govern what we willingly entertain. Paul speaks of “bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ” (2 Corinthians 10:5). Philippians directs our attention toward “whatsoever things are true,” “honest,” “just,” “pure,” and “lovely” (Philippians 4:8).

That does not require examining every wrong thought until we understand why it appeared. Sometimes examining temptation merely gives it another opportunity to speak. As the song puts it, “You don't have to read what you already know.” You don't have to open the message.

There is also a difference between an unwanted thought and one we deliberately invite back. We can rehearse resentment. We can cultivate fantasy. We can feed fear. We can repeatedly return to something we know is drawing us away from God. At that point, we are no longer merely noticing what appeared at the door; we are deciding whether to give it room.

When We Have Opened What We Should Have Deleted

Even when we recognize that we have made the wrong choice, the answer is not hopeless condemnation. Scripture says, “There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus” (Romans 8:1). That does not make sin unimportant. It directs the believer back to Christ. We confess what is truly sin, receive the mercy God has provided, and learn again to choose what belongs in a mind being shaped by Him.

So when an unwanted thought appears, do not automatically pronounce yourself guilty merely because it arrived. Recognize it. Measure it by Scripture. Bring your thinking under the obedience of Christ. And when the thought has no rightful place there, the song, Not Guilty Of Thinking, offers a simple illustration of that biblical discernment: “Delete it and let it go.”
